Part 1The project in one page10 min

Title: Cost-Effective Image-Based 3D Digitization of Cultural Heritage Artifacts for Virtual Museum Applications.

The problem in plain words. A museum can turn an object into a 3D model just by photographing it from many sides and letting software rebuild its shape. Every extra photo costs capture time, storage and, most of all, computer time. Too few photos and parts of the object come out missing or wrong. So how many photos are actually enough? Nobody had measured this carefully for single objects under controlled conditions.

What we did. We took 40 artifacts from a public dataset (CHSD). For each one we made five photo sets of decreasing size (362, 182, 92, 47, 26 photos), rebuilt the 3D model from every set with the same free software on our own laptop (no graphics card), and measured three things: how accurate the shape is, how good it looks, and how much time, memory and storage it cost. That is 200 reconstructions. Then we compressed the good models for the web and built a walkable 3D virtual museum that shows 44 of them.

The answer. 47 to 92 photos give the same quality as 362, in 6–17% of the processing time. 26 photos are too few. Compressed web models keep their accuracy at about a fifth of the file size.

Research questions (RQ) and hypotheses (H)

Say it in one line: “Same artifact, five photo budgets, same pipeline, then compare quality against cost, and deliver the good models in a web museum.”

Part 2The building blocks of 3D25 min

Learn these words first; everything later uses them.

2.1 How a computer stores a 3D object

  • Point cloud: a big list of 3D points (x, y, z), often with a colour each. Like a statue made of sand grains. No surface, just dots.
  • Mesh: a surface made of tiny triangles joined at their corners (vertices). This is what games and the web use. Our full models have about 400,000 triangles; museum models 100,000.
  • Texture: an image “painted” onto the mesh, so the surface shows the real colours and patterns. It is stored as a texture atlas, one image holding many patches.
  • Textured mesh = mesh + texture. This is our final output.
  • GLB: the binary version of glTF, the standard 3D file format for the web: mesh + texture in one file.

2.2 Ways to digitize an object

MethodHowCostUsed?
Laser / structured-light scannerSpecial device measures distances directlyExpensive hardwareNo
Photogrammetry (SfM + MVS)Many ordinary photos → software works out 3D shapeCamera + free softwareYes, this is ours
NeRF (Neural Radiance Fields)A neural network learns to render new views of the objectNeeds a GPU, training time; no clean mesh by defaultNo (out of scope)
3D Gaussian Splatting (3DGS)Millions of small coloured blobs, optimised to match the photos; very fast to viewNeeds a GPU; output is blobs, not a meshNo (out of scope)
If asked “which 3D reconstruction method did you use?” “Classical photogrammetry: structure from motion to find the cameras, multi-view stereo to get a dense point cloud, then a triangle mesh with a texture. Our final model is a textured mesh. We did not use NeRF or Gaussian Splatting: they need a GPU, and they were out of our agreed scope.”

2.3 The two big steps of photogrammetry

Photos→SfM: where was each camera?→MVS: dense 3D points→Mesh→Texture
  • Structure from Motion (SfM): looks at the photos, finds the same small details in different photos, and from that works out where each photo was taken from (camera position and angle, called the camera pose) plus a sparse set of 3D points (thousands). Done by COLMAP.
  • Multi-View Stereo (MVS): now that cameras are known, it compares photos pixel by pixel to estimate depth everywhere, giving a dense point cloud (around a million points), then a mesh and texture. Done by OpenMVS.
Analogy: SfM is like working out where every tourist stood by comparing their holiday photos of the same building. MVS is then using those known positions to measure the building's shape in detail.

2.4 Words about cameras and views

  • Azimuth: the angle around the object (like walking around it). Elevation: how high the camera is (looking down or up).
  • Overlap: how much two neighbouring photos show the same part. SfM needs overlap to match features. Too few photos means too little overlap, which is why 26 photos fail.
  • Intrinsics: the camera's own properties (focal length, image centre). Extrinsics / pose: where it is and which way it points.

Part 3How COLMAP and OpenMVS work inside25 min

This is the part examiners like to probe. Know the stage names and what each does.

3.1 COLMAP: Structure from Motion (we used the CPU build, version 4.2.1)

  1. Feature extraction (feature_extractor): finds thousands of distinctive small patches in every photo using SIFT (Scale-Invariant Feature Transform). Each feature gets a descriptor, a 128-number fingerprint, that stays similar when the patch is seen from a slightly different angle, size or light. We told COLMAP that all photos come from one camera with a simple pinhole model (SIMPLE_PINHOLE: one focal length plus image centre), because CHSD uses one ideal virtual camera.
  2. Feature matching (exhaustive_matcher): compares the fingerprints between every pair of photos to find the same points. Wrong matches are removed by geometric verification: COLMAP fits the relation between two views (the essential/fundamental matrix) with RANSAC, a method that repeatedly tries small random samples and keeps the model most matches agree with. Exhaustive matching compares n×(n−1)/2 pairs, so it grows with the square of the photo count. That is why 362 photos are so slow: 65,341 pairs against 4,186 for 92 photos.
  3. Mapping (mapper, incremental SfM):
    • Start: picks a good starting pair of photos and sets them up in 3D.
    • Register: adds one photo at a time, finding its pose from points already in 3D (a step called PnP, Perspective-n-Point).
    • Triangulate: creates new 3D points from matching features.
    • Refine: regularly runs bundle adjustment, a big optimisation that adjusts all cameras and points together so the projected points land as close as possible to where they were seen. The leftover error is the reprojection error; ours was about 0.2 pixels, which is very good.
  4. Output: camera poses + sparse point cloud. If some photos cannot be linked to the rest, COLMAP may produce two separate models (a “split”). Our pipeline then densifies only the larger one, which is a known limitation (see Part 8).
  5. Undistortion (image_undistorter): prepares images and cameras in the exact format the dense step needs.

3.2 OpenMVS: dense reconstruction, mesh, texture (version 2.4.0, CPU)

  1. InterfaceCOLMAP: converts COLMAP's output into OpenMVS's scene file.
  2. DensifyPointCloud: for each photo, picks neighbouring photos and estimates a depth map (distance of every pixel) using PatchMatch stereo. PatchMatch starts with random depth guesses, keeps the ones whose small image patches look alike across views, and spreads good guesses to neighbouring pixels. All depth maps are then fused into one dense point cloud, keeping points that several views agree on. Setting: --resolution-level 1 (half resolution, but OpenMVS keeps a 640 px minimum, so our 800×600 images stayed full size).
  3. ReconstructMesh: turns the points into a watertight surface. It builds a Delaunay tetrahedralization (fills space with tetrahedra connecting the points), then decides which tetrahedra are inside or outside the object using the camera rays that saw each point, solved as a graph cut (minimum cut). The boundary between inside and outside becomes the triangle mesh.
  4. TextureMesh: chooses, for every triangle, the best photo to take its colour from (a sharp, front-facing view, with neighbours preferring the same photo to avoid patchwork), packs the patches into a texture atlas, and can level colour differences at the seams.
A real problem we found: OpenMVS's default seam levelling turned some faces black on CHSD's black-background images. For museum and visual-quality models we re-textured with seam levelling switched off. Geometry and F-scores never used the texture, so they were unaffected.

3.3 Why COLMAP + OpenMVS, and why not COLMAP alone?

COLMAP can also do dense MVS, but only with an NVIDIA GPU (CUDA). Our laptop has Intel Iris Xe graphics, no CUDA. COLMAP's SfM runs fine on CPU; OpenMVS does the dense part on CPU and reads COLMAP's output directly. The method stays the same (SfM + MVS); only the dense tool changes.

Part 4The dataset: CHSD10 min

  • Name: Cultural Heritage Synthetic Depth Dataset (CHSD), downloaded from Zenodo (an open research data site): RGB images doi 10.5281/zenodo.11492154 (46.2 GB) and depth maps doi 10.5281/zenodo.11484461 (7.9 GB). MD5 checksums verified after download.
  • What it is: its authors took 3D models of 81 heritage artifacts from the Virtual Hampson Museum collection (pots, vessels, figurines, beads, a pipe…) and rendered virtual photos of each on a virtual turntable.
  • Per artifact: 360 positions around it (every 1°) × 5 camera heights (−90°, −45°, 0°, 45°, 90°; the ±90° rings are straight-down/up views) = 1,800 views, 800×600 px on a black background, plus a 16-bit depth map for every view. 81 × 1,800 = 145,800 files per part.
  • Licence: CC BY-NC-SA 4.0: non-commercial, give credit, share derived work (our models) under the same licence. The museum shows the credit in its corner.

Why a synthetic dataset?

A controlled experiment needs a perfect answer key. CHSD gives exact cameras and a depth map for every view, so we can measure accuracy precisely and change only the number of photos. With real photos, blur, lighting and camera errors would mix into the result. The price is that results are benchmark findings, not a guarantee for real museum photos (our main limitation).

Main set and cut-off set

  • We inspected all 81: in 41 the object is cut off by the image edge in some views (partly outside the picture). That would damage both the reconstruction and the reference.
  • Main set = the 40 never cut off, all five levels each (200 runs). Cut-off set = 19 artifacts cut off in at most 30% of sample views, run at one level (L3) only; 14 passed and are on the museum's upper floor.
Rule to remember: photos (RGB) are the only input to reconstruction. Depth maps are used only to check accuracy, never to build models.

Part 6How we measured quality and cost15 min

6.1 Shape accuracy (geometry)

  1. Reference shape: we turned all 1,800 depth maps of the artifact into 3D points (“back-projection”) and merged them: the answer key.
  2. Alignment: COLMAP's model has its own arbitrary scale and orientation. We matched it to the dataset using the camera positions (a similarity transform, Umeyama method). Cameras lined up to about 0.0003 R, far below our tolerance.
  3. Unit R: distance from camera to object centre; objects are about 0.3–0.8 R across. Using R makes all artifacts comparable.
  4. F-score at 0.002 R: precision = share of our points within 0.002 R of the true surface (“is what we built in the right place?”); recall = share of the true surface we covered within 0.002 R (“did we build all of it?”). F = 2·P·R / (P + R), from 0 to 1. Checked also at 0.005 R and 0.01 R. This is the standard summary in 3D benchmarks such as Tanks and Temples.
  5. Also: Chamfer distance (average distance both ways) and Hausdorff 95% (near-worst distance). Main measure: mesh F, because the museum shows the mesh.

6.2 Appearance (visual)

We rendered each model from 12 held-out views (angles no photo set used) and compared with the dataset image: PSNR (pixel difference in decibels, higher is better, about 29 dB here), SSIM (structural similarity 0–1, how alike edges and textures look) and silhouette IoU (overlap of outlines).

6.3 Cost

Processing time, peak memory (RAM) and storage per run, all measured on the laptop.

6.4 Statistics (why the numbers are trustworthy)

  • Paired comparison: levels compared on the same 40 artifacts.
  • Wilcoxon signed-rank test: checks whether one level is consistently better than another without assuming normal data.
  • Holm correction: adjusts p-values because we did several comparisons.
  • Bootstrap 95% interval: resamples the artifacts 10,000 times to show how certain each median difference is.
  • Sufficiency bar: mesh F ≥ 0.95, fixed before analysis (also the museum's exhibit rule).

6.5 The combined cost-effectiveness score

Score = w·Q + (1 − w)·S, with w = 0.5

Q (quality) = mean of median mesh F, median SSIM and the share of artifacts that pass. S (saving) = 1 − mean of time, RAM and storage as a share of L1. L1 saves nothing (S = 0). The weights are our choice, so we recomputed the ranking for every w from 0 to 1: L4 wins for w 0.15–0.80, L3 for 0.85–0.95. The conclusion does not depend on the weights.

Part 7The virtual museum and its code20 min

Built from scratch by us in JavaScript on three.js, a free open-source WebGL library (WebGL = the browser's 3D graphics). three.js is the engine; the museum is our own design.

7.1 From model to exhibit

L3 mesh (≈400k triangles)→decimate to 100k→re-texture, JPEG ≤ 2048 px→GLB file ≈ 3 MB→artifacts.json list→museum

Exhibit rule: an artifact is shown only if its L3 mesh F ≥ 0.95. The Python script export_glb.py does all of this and writes museum/artifacts.json (each exhibit's name, file and measured facts for its placard).

7.2 The JavaScript files (components)

layout.jsThe floor plan, sized from the number of exhibits: domed rotunda + four galleries, a staircase and an upper gallery for the cut-off set; gives the floor height anywhere (for stairs).

7.3 Performance

60 frames per second with all 44 exhibits on the laptop's integrated graphics (up to about 4.3 million triangles on screen). Whole collection: 129.6 MB; nearest models load first so the hall is usable within seconds.

7.4 How to run it

It is a static website (no server code). In the museum folder run python -m http.server 8000 and open http://localhost:8000 (or double-click Deliverables/Demo/Run_Museum.bat). Opening index.html by double-click does not work (browsers block local model loading). Needs internet for three.js.

Three findings to explain in your own words

  • Threshold: quality is flat from 362 down to 47 photos, then collapses at 26 (14 of 40 runs produced no dense cloud).
  • Surprise: more photos made the dense cloud slightly less precise at the tight tolerance (0.98 at L4 vs 0.80 at L1). Likely cause (not proven): many closely spaced views add slightly misplaced points that the fixed settings don't filter. The mesh is unaffected.

Limitations (say them before you are asked)

  • Synthetic images (ideal cameras, lighting).
  • One pipeline, one laptop; each run timed once.
  • Split SfM models: only the largest densified.
  • Cut-off set only at L3.
  • Combined-score weights are our choice (checked over all weights).
  • Museum not hosted online yet; download speed simulated.

Part 9Literature, gap, novelty, methodology10 min

Literature review

29 papers (2011–2026) in an Excel workbook (docs/literature/…FINAL.xlsx), in four themes: artifact digitization, image-based reconstruction, low-cost digitization, virtual museums. For each: problem, method, data, metrics, findings, limitation. Mostly international peer-reviewed venues (ISPRS Archives, Elsevier, MDPI, Springer Nature, PLOS ONE, 3 IEEE conference papers). What they show: digitization works, SfM-MVS is mature and cheap, low-cost capture is feasible, web museums work. What they mostly do: add hardware, compare methods/devices, focus on sites or user experience.

Research gap

Image count is rarely varied systematically for single artifacts, and acquisition effort, computing cost and quality are seldom measured together on the same objects, nor carried through to web delivery.

Novelty

Say: “Not a new algorithm. Our novelty is a controlled, reproducible framework that links photo effort and computing cost to shape and appearance quality, per artifact, with statistics and a combined score, carried through to a working virtual museum.” Do not claim “first ever”.

Part 11Your questions, answered20 min

Grouped in the order a panel usually goes: data and background, method, results, then you, then the future.

Where did you get the dataset?
From Zenodo: the Cultural Heritage Synthetic Depth Dataset (CHSD), two records (RGB images and depth maps, DOIs in Part 4), licence CC BY-NC-SA 4.0. It contains 81 artifacts from the Virtual Hampson Museum collection, 1,800 rendered views each. We verified the download with checksums and inspected every artifact.
Show your literature review.
Open the Excel workbook (docs/literature/Literature_Review_Cultural_Heritage_Artifacts_FINAL.xlsx): 29 papers in four themes with method, metrics, findings and limitations, plus a gap matrix, the top-10 papers and our final project definition. Report Chapter 2 summarises it; slide 5 compares six closest works.
What are the research gaps?
Existing work proves low-cost digitization, quality measurement and web museums each work, but rarely varies the number of photos systematically for single artifacts, and rarely measures photo effort, computing cost and quality together on the same objects, carried through to web delivery.
What is the novelty?
A controlled, reproducible framework, not a new algorithm: same artifact, five photo budgets, same pipeline; shape, appearance and cost measured together with statistics and a combined score; results carried into a working virtual museum with measured browser costs.
Proposed vs actual methodology?
Same core (photo subsets → SfM + MVS → evaluate → web museum). Changes: OpenMVS instead of COLMAP's dense step (no CUDA), levels defined by azimuth step, 40 + 19 artifacts, added visual fidelity, browser cost and a combined score. Table in Part 9.
What is your tech stack?
Reconstruction: COLMAP (SfM) + OpenMVS (dense, mesh, texture), both on CPU. Analysis: Python with NumPy, SciPy, Matplotlib, tested with pytest. Museum: JavaScript with three.js (WebGL), GLB models, static website. Git/GitHub for version control.
Why did some artifacts not pass the museum score (mesh F ≥ 0.95 at L3)?
10 of 40 main-set and 5 of 19 cut-off artifacts failed. Two causes:
  1. Split reconstructions: SfM could not link all photos into one model and made two. Our pipeline densifies only the larger, so one side of the object is missing (e.g. Ark_HM_341, 31, 797, 703; three cut-off failures).
  2. Small, plain, low-texture objects: too few distinctive features to match (e.g. Ark_HM_1260B, 1260C, 38). This is our explanation; we did not test it separately.
One (Ark_HM_792) registered all photos but still scored low; we did not find the cause. More photos did not rescue them: 9 of 40 never pass even with 362.
What are your numbers? key
47–92 photos match 362 in quality at 6–17% of the time; 26 photos break down (mesh F 0.58). L4 has the best combined score, 0.82. Museum models: 19% of file size, 23% of download time, 43% of drawing time, −0.2 dB PSNR. 44 exhibits at 60 fps. Table in Part 8.
What are your limitations?
Synthetic images; one pipeline and one laptop; each run timed once; split SfM models; cut-off set only at L3; combined-score weights are our choice (we checked all weights); museum not yet hosted, download speed simulated.
If I photograph an artifact with my phone, can you turn it into a 3D model?
Yes, with the same pipeline: COLMAP works out the phone's camera itself. Our measured times are for 800×600 images (92 photos ≈ 10 min, 362 ≈ 55 min). Phone photos have about 25× more pixels, so on this laptop we would expect roughly 1–4 hours for 50–100 photos at full resolution, or much less if we resize them first. That is an estimate; we have not run real phone photos. Tips: walk around the object in even steps (~24°, at two or three heights), even light, plain background, no motion blur. We could not compute an F-score for it, because there is no reference shape for a real object.
About your draft answer “3.5–4 hours”: fine as an upper estimate for many full-resolution photos, but say it is an estimate and depends on photo count and resolution.
Why not use a GPU? Cost-effectiveness or resource limitation?
Both, and they point the same way. Our laptop has Intel Iris Xe graphics, not an NVIDIA GPU, so CUDA-based tools (COLMAP's dense step, NeRF, 3DGS training) cannot run. And the project's aim is cost-effective digitization for small museums and colleges, which usually have exactly this kind of hardware. Showing it works on a CPU laptop is part of the result. A GPU would mainly make the dense step faster (likely, not measured by us).
How will you deploy the museum?
It is a static website (HTML, JavaScript, 44 GLB files ≈ 130 MB, each ≈ 3 MB), so any static host works: GitHub Pages (publish the museum folder), Netlify (drag-and-drop the folder) or a university web server. No backend or database is needed; three.js loads from a CDN. The CC BY-NC-SA licence allows this, non-commercially and with the credit shown. Right now it runs locally for the demo; hosting is a next step.
How much did you do yourselves, and how much was AI?
Answer honestly, matching the declaration in the report. We used an AI coding assistant (Claude Code) to write and test most of the code, run the batches, build the analysis and the museum, and draft documents. We decided the problem, research questions, dataset, method and scope; made every design decision (levels, exhibit rule, combined score, museum features); checked results and edited the documents. Every number comes from runs on our laptop, with logs. Then show you understand it, using this guide.
Do not claim you wrote everything by hand: the report's declaration states AI use, and a mismatch is worse than the honest answer.
What is the split of work between you two?
No split is recorded in our project files: both of you directed every session together on the same laptop, and commits alternate between you. Agree tonight on a truthful split by area and both say the same thing. Example framing: “We worked together throughout; I led ___, she led ___.” Use the same split as in the presentation.
What did you learn?
How 3D reconstruction works (SfM, MVS, meshing, texturing); how to design a fair experiment (one variable, frozen settings, paired comparison); evaluation metrics (F-score, Chamfer, PSNR, SSIM) and statistics (Wilcoxon, Holm, bootstrap); 3D on the web (three.js, GLB, performance); running long reproducible experiments with Git, logs and tests.
How will you use this in the future?
As capture guidelines for real collections (≈50–90 photos per object, even spacing); digitizing a college or local museum collection on ordinary laptops; validating with real phone photos; hosting the museum; extending to neural methods (NeRF/3DGS) on the same controlled subsets; possibly a paper.

Part 12More questions the panel may ask15 min

Why is L4 better than L1 on the dense cloud? More data should help.
The mesh is equally good from L1 to L4. Only the dense cloud's precision falls with more photos at the tight 0.002 R tolerance; at 0.01 R all score ≥ 0.998. Likely cause: closely spaced views add slightly misplaced points (hypothesis).
Why does L5 fail?
At 45° steps neighbouring photos overlap too little, so features cannot be matched, cameras fail to register and the dense step has too few neighbours. 14 of 40 L5 runs gave no dense cloud.
Which level do you recommend?
L4 (47 photos) for the lowest cost with the same quality; L3 (92 photos) if reliability matters more, since it loses two fewer objects. Nothing beyond L2 helped.
Why is the museum built from L3, not L4?
L3 had no extra failures when the museum rule was set, and a museum needs reliability across many objects. The 30 main-set exhibits are exactly the 30 that pass at L3.
What is F-score in one line?
Harmonic mean of precision (built in the right place) and recall (built all of it) at a distance tolerance; 1 is perfect.
How do you know the evaluation is correct?
The reference comes from independent depth maps, never from our model; alignment uses known cameras (residual 0.0003 R); failed reconstructions score near zero as expected; 81 unit tests cover the metrics.
Why not use more artifacts, or all 81?
41 are cut off by the frame in some views, which would make the comparison unfair. The 40 clean ones gave enough pairs for statistics; 19 cut-off ones were tested separately.
Why these weights in the combined score?
Equal weight is the neutral choice; we showed the winner for every weight from 0 to 1. L4 or L3 wins across 0.15–0.95.
What does decimation mean?
Reducing the number of triangles while keeping the shape: about 400k → 100k. Our measurement shows shape accuracy changed by at most 0.0003 in F-score.
What does “cost-effective” mean here? You never measured money.
Cost = effort and resources: number of photos (capture effort), processing time, memory and storage, all on free software and an ordinary laptop. Budget: ₹0.
Would this work for real objects?
The pattern (a plateau, then a breakdown) is likely to hold, but exact numbers may shift with real blur and lighting. Testing with real photos is the next step.
What is the 3D model made of, technically?
A triangle mesh (vertices + faces) with UV texture coordinates and one JPEG texture atlas, stored as GLB; about 100k triangles and 3 MB per exhibit.
Why is the museum fast on a laptop?
Compressed models, merged building geometry (few draw calls), procedural textures, nearest-first loading, and simple lighting.
