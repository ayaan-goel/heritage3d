1The project in one minute

Say this first. It frames everything else.

Question: how many photos do you really need to digitize a heritage artifact well enough for preservation and a virtual museum, and what does each extra photo cost in computing?

What we did: took the same artifacts from the CHSD dataset, made five photo sets of decreasing size (362 → 26 images), ran every set through the same free, CPU-only pipeline (COLMAP + OpenMVS) on our own laptop, measured shape accuracy, appearance, time, memory and storage, and put the passing models into a walkable 3D web museum.

Answer: 47–92 photos give the same quality as 362, at 6–17% of the computing time. Fewer than that (26) breaks down. The museum's compressed models look the same as the full ones at a fifth of the size.

RQ1 · Quality answered

Quality behaves like a threshold, not a slope. From 362 down to 47 images, shape and appearance stay the same; at 26 images coverage breaks and both collapse (14 of 40 runs make no dense cloud).

Surprise worth mentioning: more images made the dense cloud slightly less precise at the tight tolerance (cause not established).

RQ2 · Trade-off answered

Time grows faster than the image count (about images1.34) but quality stops improving at 47 images. For all 40 artifacts: L4 2.7 h (28 pass), L3 6.3 h (30 pass), L1 40.1 h (31 pass).

Combined score: L4 best (0.82) with equal weights; L4 or L3 best for quality weights from 0.15 to 0.95.

RQ3 · Museum models answered

Compressed models (100k triangles) keep the same shape accuracy and lose 0.2 dB PSNR, at 19% of the file size. In the browser: download 23%, decoding 31%, drawing 43% of the full model's time.

Whole collection at 20 Mbit/s: 68 s against 281 s.

Hypotheses

H1 supported fewer images cut cost; too few (L5) cut quality. Memory stops falling below L3.

H2 supported 47–92 images match 362 (no formal equivalence margin was set).

H3 supported optimized models keep fidelity at a fraction of the cost.

Novelty: how to word it

"Earlier studies show that low-cost photogrammetry works, or measure quality, or report runtime, mostly separately and often for sites rather than single objects. We measure acquisition effort, computing cost and quality together, on the same artifacts, under one controlled, reproducible setup, and carry the result through to a working virtual museum. The novelty is the framework and its integration, not a new reconstruction algorithm."

Source: the literature workbook, sheets "Gap Matrix" and "Gap RQ Novelty". Do not claim a new algorithm or "first ever"; claim the controlled, joint analysis.

5Limitations to state before she asks

Raising them ourselves shows we understand the work.

  • Synthetic data. CHSD images are renders with perfect cameras and lighting. Results are benchmark findings, not proof for real museum photos.
  • One pipeline, one laptop. One frozen COLMAP + OpenMVS setting; each run timed once, not repeated.
  • Split models. In some artifacts COLMAP splits the photos into two separate models and we only densify the larger one, so part of the object is missing. Kept as a reported finding; fixing it would change the frozen pipeline.
  • Hard objects. 9 of 40 artifacts never pass at any level (small, plain, low-texture objects and the split models).
  • The combined score's weights are our choice. That is why we show the weight check and keep the full tables.
  • Museum not hosted yet; download times use a simulated 20 Mbit/s connection. Full-screen performance not measured yet.

7Questions she may ask, with answers

Click a question to see the answer. In the PDF all answers are open. Keep answers short, then offer the detail.

Motivation and novelty
What is new here? Photogrammetry of artifacts has been done many times.

Yes, the reconstruction itself is established. What is less studied is a controlled, per-artifact analysis of how many photos are enough, linked to computing cost and quality together, under one reproducible setup, carried through to a web museum. That gap is in our literature review's gap matrix. We do not claim a new algorithm.

Who benefits from this?

Small museums and colleges with a normal camera and a laptop: the result says around 50–90 photos per object are enough, which saves capture time and hours of processing per object, and the web-museum part shows how to publish the models cheaply.

Where is the "cost" in cost-effective? You did not measure money.

Cost here is effort and resources: the number of photos (capture effort), processing time, peak memory and storage, all on low-cost hardware with free software. We did not convert these to rupees; the number of photos stands in for capture time.

Dataset
Why a synthetic dataset and not real photos?

Because a controlled experiment needs exact ground truth. CHSD gives exact cameras and a depth map for every view, so we can measure accuracy precisely and change only the number of photos. With real photos, lighting, blur and camera errors would mix into the result. The cost is generalisation, which we state as a limitation.

Why only 40 of the 81 artifacts in the main set?

41 of the 81 are cut off by the image frame in some views, which would damage both the reconstruction and the reference. The 40 that are never cut off form the main set. We also ran the 19 least cut-off ones at L3 as a separate set: 14 passed and are on the museum's upper floor.

Are the depth maps used to build the models?

No. Reconstruction uses RGB images only. Depth maps are used only to build the reference for measuring accuracy.

Method
Why COLMAP and OpenMVS, not Meshroom, Metashape or RealityCapture?

They are free, open-source, scriptable and run on a CPU. Our laptop has no NVIDIA GPU, and COLMAP's own dense step needs CUDA, so OpenMVS does the dense part on the CPU. Metashape and RealityCapture are proprietary. Comparing software was out of scope.

Why not NeRF or Gaussian Splatting?

They were explicitly out of scope: they need a GPU, and the aim is a low-cost, established pipeline. Our literature review covers them as background.

How were the photo subsets chosen? Is it fair to other levels?

Each level takes every n-th azimuth on all five elevation rings (steps 3°, 6°, 12°, 24°, 45°), so coverage stays even all round; the two straight up/down views are always kept. Same artifact, same settings, only the number of photos changes.

What is the F-score, and why 0.002 R?

It combines precision (how much of the model is close to the true surface) and recall (how much of the true surface the model covers), at a distance tolerance. It is the usual measure in multi-view stereo benchmarks such as Tanks and Temples. 0.002 R is a tight tolerance, about 0.25% of the pilot object's size; we also report 0.005 R and 0.01 R.

How do you line up your model with the reference?

Through the cameras: COLMAP's camera positions are aligned to CHSD's known camera positions. In the pilot the cameras lined up to within 0.00025 R on average, much smaller than the 0.002 R tolerance.

Why the threshold 0.95, and why the mesh, not the point cloud?

The mesh is what the museum shows. The 0.95 bar was fixed on 31 October, before the trade-off analysis. Changing it to 0.90 or 0.98 changes little: L4 stays the cheapest passing level for most artifacts.

Why Wilcoxon tests?

The comparisons are paired (same artifact at two levels), the differences are not normally distributed, and there are 40 pairs, so a non-parametric paired test fits. Holm's correction handles several comparisons at once.

Results
Why does quality not improve with more photos? Isn't more data always better?

On the mesh, quality is already near its ceiling at 47 photos. On the dense cloud, more photos actually lowered precision at the tight tolerance (0.981 at L4 vs 0.795 at L1), while coverage stayed the same. Our hypothesis is that many closely spaced views add slightly misplaced points that the fixed OpenMVS settings do not filter. We have not proven the cause. At a looser tolerance (0.01 R) every level from L1 to L4 scores 0.998 or more.

Why does L5 fail?

At 45° between photos, neighbouring images overlap too little to match features, so cameras fail to register and the dense step has too few neighbours. 14 of 40 L5 runs produced no dense cloud; recall falls to 0.41.

So which level do you recommend?

L4 (47 photos) is the most economical: same median quality at 6.3% of L1's time. L3 (92 photos) is the safer choice: it loses two fewer artifacts for about twice L4's time. Beyond L2 nothing was gained.

Why did 9 artifacts never pass, even with 362 photos?

Two causes: COLMAP splitting the photos into separate models (only the larger is densified, so one side is missing), and small, plain, low-texture objects with few features to match (the second is a hypothesis). More photos did not rescue them.

Why is the museum built from L3 and not L4?

L3 was the lowest level with no extra breakdowns when the museum rule was fixed (after round 1), and the museum needs reliability across many objects. The rule is L3 mesh F ≥ 0.95; the 30 main-set exhibits are exactly the 30 artifacts that pass at L3.

How was the combined score built? Aren't the weights arbitrary?

Score = half quality + half saving. Quality is the average of median mesh F, median SSIM and the share of artifacts that pass; saving is 1 minus the average of time, memory and storage relative to L1. The weights are a choice, which is why we recomputed it for every weighting from 0 to 1: L4 wins from 0.15 to 0.80, L3 from 0.85 to 0.95. The conclusion does not depend on the exact weights.

Are your times reliable? Did you run anything else at the same time?

Runs went in overnight batches with a guard that discarded any level where the laptop slept or ran on battery. Each level ran once, so there is some run-to-run noise; the differences between levels (up to 55×) are far larger than that.

Virtual museum
How did you make the models web-ready?

Each L3 mesh is reduced to about 100,000 triangles and its texture saved as JPEG of at most 2048 px, packed as a GLB file. Median 15.7 MB → 2.9 MB, with no measurable loss of shape accuracy (at most 0.0003 in F-score) and 0.2 dB PSNR.

What is it built with? Will it run on a phone?

A static website with three.js (WebGL), no server-side code. It held 60 fps on the laptop's integrated Intel graphics. It has not been tested on phones yet, and it is not hosted online yet.

Is the building a real campus building?

The gate scene models the PDEU main gate; the museum building itself is our own design, laid out from the hall's real floor plan. It is not a real campus building.

Limitations and future work
Would these results hold for real photos?

The trend (a plateau and a breakdown point) is likely to hold, but the exact numbers may shift with real lighting, blur and camera errors. That is a hypothesis; testing it with real photos is the natural next step.

What would you do with more time?

A small real-photo test; densifying both halves of split models; trying a cheaper feature matcher to cut L1's matching time; hosting the museum and measuring real download times; a small user study (out of scope now).

What did you write yourselves, and how do you know it is correct?

The subset generator, batch runner, evaluation (alignment, reference, F-scores), the visual renderer, the statistics, the GLB export and the whole museum. 81 automatic tests cover the core code, and every number is traceable to a committed result file and log.
